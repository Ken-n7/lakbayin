# A small relay halves classroom lag

*Research report for NumXventure (Godot 4.6, Android phones, Windows teacher laptop, Supabase). Prepared 8 October 2026.*

NumXventure's ~1.1-second delay on other heroes is not a bug that can be tuned away. Any smooth-playback scheme produces that delay when each phone reports its path only every 0.5 s, and the 0.5 s interval exists because Supabase Realtime counts, caps and bills every message. Production games show other players 0.1-0.2 s late by sending 5-30 updates a second. For 40 phones going through Supabase, that would be about 800 messages a second: above the Pro plan's 500/s ceiling, and roughly $100-300 a month with the spend cap off. **The best payoff for the effort is to move only the walking traffic onto a small WebSocket relay that bills by compute instead of by message.** Supabase keeps accounts, questions, answers, gems and session state. The project's parked LAN host already does the job: 160 lines of GDScript that served 40 simulated phones at 10 snapshots a second with 100% delivery on 8.5% of one laptop core. It can run headless on a ~$3-6/month Singapore server, or the same design can run free on a Cloudflare Durable Object. Combined with packed binary snapshots, this should bring the visible delay down to **roughly 0.3-0.5 s**, cut Supabase Realtime traffic by **more than 95%**, take the teacher's laptop out of the movement path, and cost about **one working week**, with today's teacher hub kept as the automatic fallback. **Before any of that, open the Supabase dashboard.** The docs put the Free plan at 100 messages/s and 2 million messages a month, yet this project ran cleanly at 165-350 messages/s, and a single 1-hour class at today's rate uses about 600,000 messages. WebSocket over port 443 is still the right transport for unknown school Wi-Fi and Philippine mobile data. Every student can keep seeing every other student walk, because full fidelity costs cents once movement is off a per-message meter.

## Where things stand: a smooth hub, a 1.1 s lag, and an unclear plan

The user's original brief described a design that no longer ships, so the numbers this report builds on come first. All were measured in this project in October 2026 (project load tests, `MERGE_CHECKLIST.md` "Server load", and the `lan-classroom` branch).

**Original design.** Each phone broadcast about 7 positions a second as JSON on one shared Supabase Realtime Broadcast channel, and other phones interpolated 0.25 s behind. With 30 walkers, **only 6-17% of messages were delivered**, and Supabase cut phones off with "Too many messages per second". Per-team channels did not help, because the limit applies to the whole project. Traffic stayed clean at ~260-350 messages/s and was cut off above ~520.

**Current build: the teacher hub.** Each phone samples its position every 0.1 s and sends its recorded path as a bundle every 0.5 s. Each bundle overlaps the previous one by 0.3 s, a phone standing still sends one every 3 s, and a phone answering a question sends nothing. Bundles go on the phone's own channel, which only the teacher's Game Controls screen joins. The teacher sends one snapshot of everyone who moved three times a second, with changed progress rows included, and skips ticks when nothing changed. Phones play others back through an adaptive jitter buffer that speeds up or slows down instead of jumping. Measured with 30 students walking and answering for 90 s:

| Bundle interval | Messages/s | Playback delay | Delivered |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 s | ~125 | ~1.5-2.1 s | |
| **0.5 s (current)** | **~165** | **~1.0-1.2 s** | **98%** |
| 0.33 s | ~195 | ~0.85-1.0 s | |
| 0.25 s | ~220 | | only 76% of motion shown smoothly |

Snapshots were about 2.6 KB at 30 players. Answer round trips had a p95 of ~310-370 ms. The hub's transport lag (phone → Supabase → teacher laptop → Supabase → phone) measured a median of 326 ms and a p95 of 485-514 ms. The estimate for 40 walkers is ~185 messages/s with 0.5 s bundles and ~285 with 0.33 s. On a real phone the user found the current build **smooth, but the delay feels too long**. The client requires the game to be online, with all students on one map seeing each other.

**The parked LAN spike.** A Godot WebSocket host on the teacher's laptop sent 10 snapshots a second. It served **40 simulated phones at 100% delivery, 387 messages/s out, p95 lag 111 ms, on 8.5% of one CPU core**. At 80 phones it reached 95.6% delivery on 13.5% of a core (project measurement, `lan-classroom` commit 46f81d8). The client rejected LAN because they have no hotspot that can handle 30+ devices, and the school Wi-Fi quality is unknown.

> **Check this first: the measured limits do not match the documented Free plan.** Supabase documents the plans as follows. **Free**: 100 messages/s, 200 concurrent connections, 2 M messages a month. **Pro**: 500 messages/s, 500 connections, 5 M messages a month, $25 plus $2.50 per extra million. **Pro with the spend cap off**: 2,500 messages/s ([Supabase Realtime limits](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/realtime/limits); [Supabase Realtime pricing](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/realtime/pricing)). Both directions count: an event is "a WebSocket message delivered to, or sent from a client" ([Supabase limits, md](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/realtime/limits.md)). This project's own checklist labels its load tests "free tier", yet it measured clean operation at 165-350 messages/s and a cut-off near ~520. **That matches Pro, not Free.** Either the project is on Pro, or its Free limit is set higher than documented (the docs say limits are "configurable per project"). The monthly numbers matter just as much. At ~165 messages/s, **one 1-hour class is about 600,000 messages**, so Free's 2 M covers about 3.4 class-hours a month and Pro's 5 M about 8.4. With Pro's default spend cap, usage past the quota is "disallowed until the next billing cycle" ([Supabase cost control](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/platform/cost-control)). Supabase also warns that projects that "consistently exceed" limits "by a significant margin" may be suspended by hand (`RealtimeDisabledForTenant`), and only support can restore them ([Supabase: project suspended](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/troubleshooting/realtime-project-suspended-for-exceeding-quotas.md)). **Action: in the dashboard, check the plan, the Realtime Messages used this billing cycle, peak connections, and the project's region.** *Unverified:* the docs do not say whether Free is hard-blocked after 2 M messages, and two Supabase pages disagree on how hard Pro's spend cap stops traffic.

## The lag is the price of a half-second send interval

A remote hero moves smoothly only if your phone always holds enough of that hero's path to keep playing until the next piece arrives. Today a piece arrives every 0.5 s. It then crosses the internet four times and waits up to 0.33 s in the teacher's hub, and the project measured that trip at roughly 0.3-0.5 s. Add 0.5 s of interval to ~0.5 s of trip and you get about 1.0 s, which is exactly the delay measured. Published rules give the same answer. Valve's Source engine renders others two snapshot intervals in the past (100 ms at ~20 snapshots/s) so that "even if one snapshot is lost, there are always two valid snapshots to interpolate between" ([Valve, Source Multiplayer Networking](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Source_Multiplayer_Networking)). Glenn Fiedler's rule is to buffer enough "that I can lose two packets in a row", about **three send intervals plus a frame or two of jitter**, or ~350 ms at 10 packets/s ([Gaffer On Games, Snapshot Interpolation](https://gafferongames.com/post/snapshot_interpolation/)). Applied to a 0.5 s interval, these rules predict **1.0-1.5 s**. The 0.33 s bundles predicted 0.67-1.0 s and measured 0.85-1.0 s. Retuning the buffer can shave tenths of a second. Only a shorter interval removes the delay.

| Game or engine | Updates each client receives | Delay on other players | How it saves traffic | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Overwatch (PC) | 21/s raised to 60/s | ~60 ms, now ~20 ms | Automatically drops back to the lower rate on weak connections | [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/overwatch-netcode-tick-rate-60hz/) |

The table makes the gap plain. Kids' social worlds settled on 5-10 updates a second and 0.1-0.2 s of display lag. NumXventure's phones report two to five times less often, and its lag is five to ten times longer. On Supabase, closing that gap is a billing problem rather than a technical one. Running ~10 Hz end to end for 40 phones means ~400 uploads plus 10 × 41 snapshot deliveries, **about 810 messages/s**. That is over Pro's 500/s and is allowed only on Pro with the spend cap off ([Supabase limits](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/realtime/limits)), at an estimated **$98-300/month** for 33-66 class-hours ([Supabase pricing](https://supabase.com/pricing); arithmetic in the research notes). The same traffic, packed in binary, is only ~1 Mbit/s of server output. **The constraint is the meter, not the bandwidth.**

Guessing ahead looks like a free alternative but is not. The project already tried sending single positions and extrapolating between them, and it "made the heroes jump whenever a real thumb weaved" (client feedback, 8 Oct 2026, recorded in `AdventureScreen.gd`). The literature predicts this. Gambetta writes that players who "run, stop, and turn corners" make dead reckoning "essentially useless" ([Gambetta, Entity Interpolation](https://www.gabrielgambetta.com/entity-interpolation.html)). Pantel and Wolf found sports-style movement the hardest to predict, with 25.6% residual error at 200 ms ([Pantel & Wolf 2002](https://svn.sable.mcgill.ca/sable/courses/COMP763/oldpapers/pantel-02-suitability.pdf)). NumXventure uses an analog virtual joystick with wall-sliding, which is exactly that case.

How low does the delay need to go? Claypool and Claypool put the point where latency starts to hurt **a player's own actions** at 100 ms for first-person games, 500 ms for third-person avatar games and 1,000 ms for "omnipresent" games ([Claypool & Claypool, WPI TR-06-13](https://ftp.cs.wpi.edu/pub/techreports/pdf/06-13.pdf)). For other players' avatars the tolerance is far looser: Gambetta notes that a 100 ms delay on others "isn't generally noticeable". Age of Empires testers did not notice 250 ms of command latency and found 250-500 ms "very playable" ([Bettner & Terrano, 1500 Archers](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/1500-archers-on-a-28-8-network-programming-in-age-of-empires-and-beyond)). No study measures noticeable delay on classmates' avatars when the players sit side by side. That situation plausibly explains the user's reaction, though: a student can watch a neighbour's real screen reach a tower a full second before that neighbour's hero arrives on their own screen. This is an inference, not a finding. **A target of ~0.3-0.5 s** sits inside every published tolerance and removes most of that visible mismatch.

## A compute-billed relay beats every per-message service

The architecture choice comes down to how each service bills. Services that bill per delivered message (Supabase, Ably, Pusher) charge a broadcast to 40 phones as 41 messages ([Supabase, Realtime messages usage](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/platform/manage-your-usage/realtime-messages); [Ably message counting](https://ably.com/docs/platform/pricing/message-counting); [Pusher pricing](https://pusher.com/channels/pricing/)). Faster movement therefore costs proportionally more. Services that bill by compute, bandwidth or connections are almost indifferent to send rate at classroom scale. Across 40 phones, 3 sessions a day and 22 school days, the research estimated **$50-315/month** for the per-message services and **$0-40/month** for the compute-billed ones.

**Why the headless Godot relay comes first for a solo GDScript developer.** Since Godot 4, any export runs as a dedicated server with `--headless`. "Export as dedicated server" with Strip Visuals removes textures from the server build ([Godot docs: dedicated servers](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/export/exporting_for_dedicated_servers.html)). The project already has the server. `LanHost.gd` is a 160-line `TCPServer` + `WebSocketPeer` host that sends one snapshot of everyone who moved every 0.1 s, encoded once. It already runs headless through `tools/lan_host.gd`, and `tools/sim/lan_load.py --host` can load-test it at any address. It uses hand-rolled snapshots rather than Godot's high-level RPC sync. That matters: a public Godot 4.4 test that used `MultiplayerSynchronizer`/RPC saw "mass disconnects" at 80-100 clients on a 2-core VM ([Godot Forum MMO demo](https://forum.godotengine.org/t/our-experience-building-an-mmo-tech-demo-with-godot-4-4-1-net-c/134348)). An idle headless Godot 4.6 process measured ~72 MB of RAM in a local test during this research, and no published benchmark exists. So a 256 MB instance should fit, but that needs checking.

**Hosting.** Fly.io has a Singapore region (`sin`). A shared-cpu-1x machine with 256 MB costs $2.19/month and Asia-Pacific egress costs $0.04/GB. Fly terminates TLS at its edge, so phones get `wss://` with no certificate work ([Fly.io pricing](https://docs.fly.io/about/pricing); [Fly.io regions](https://docs.fly.io/reference/regions); [Fly.io WebSockets](https://fly.io/blog/websockets-and-fly/)). Fly has two catches. First, new organisations get no free allowance, only a 2-hour or 7-day trial. Second, a shared vCPU is guaranteed only **6.25% of a core**, with a burst balance of up to 500 s ([Fly CPU performance](https://docs.fly.io/machines/cpu-performance)). At the 8.5% load measured for 40 phones, that balance would last roughly 6 hours of continuous play (my arithmetic). A cloud vCPU may be slower than the laptop core the 8.5% was measured on, so **load-test 40 phones on the real instance before a class**. Railway Hobby ($5 including $5 of usage, with a Singapore region) and a DigitalOcean $4-6 droplet in Singapore are the fallback hosts. Singapore is the obvious region: average pings from Manila are 28.9 ms to Singapore, 158 ms to Tokyo, ~170 ms to the US West Coast and 235 ms to Frankfurt ([WonderNetwork](https://wondernetwork.com/pings/Manila)). Every free US or EU option adds roughly 0.14-0.21 s to every round trip. The same pings matter for Supabase: the hub crosses the internet four times per position, so if the Supabase project sits outside Southeast Asia, much of today's 0.3-0.5 s trip is distance.

**The $0 alternative: a Cloudflare Durable Object.** Cloudflare's free plan includes 100,000 requests a day. **Incoming WebSocket messages are billed at 20:1, and "there is no charge for outgoing WebSocket messages"** ([Cloudflare DO pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/platform/pricing/)). Snapshots out to 40 phones therefore cost nothing. Uploads from 40 phones at 10 Hz count as ~72,000 billable requests per class-hour, so the free plan covers ~1.4 class-hours a day if everyone walks constantly. Sending only while moving, at 5 bundles a second, stretches that to ~5 class-hours a day. Beyond that, Workers Paid starts at ~$5/month (my arithmetic from the listed rates). The costs are a second language (TypeScript), a second copy of the snapshot codec, and the unverified question of where Cloudflare places the object relative to the Philippines. PartyKit, which Cloudflare acquired, now runs as this same Durable Objects model on your own account ([Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-acquires-partykit)).

**Why not a game backend?** Photon's free plan stops at 20 concurrent users, so 41 connections need the $95/year 100-CCU plan ([Photon pricing](https://www.photonengine.com/pricing)). Its Godot path, the Fusion Godot SDK, "is a development preview and is not intended for production use" ([Photon Fusion Godot SDK](https://doc.photonengine.com/fusion-godot/current/getting-started/sdk-download)). Colyseus Cloud starts at $15 with no free tier, and its Godot GDExtension "is in beta, and may not be stable" ([Colyseus docs: Godot](https://docs.colyseus.io/getting-started/godot)). Nakama is free to self-host but needs its own CockroachDB or Postgres ([Nakama Docker install](https://heroiclabs.com/docs/nakama/getting-started/install/docker/)), and its Godot client's last tagged release is v3.4.0 from March 2024 ([nakama-godot](https://github.com/heroiclabs/nakama-godot)). Each of these is a second backend beside Supabase, and Hathora's shutdown on 5 May 2026, which took Stormgate's multiplayer modes with it, shows the vendor risk ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/111969-stormgate-servers-go-dark-following-ai-focused-hosting.html)). Ably adds a specific blocker: a **50 messages/s per-connection limit** ([Ably limits](https://ably.com/docs/platform/pricing/limits)). The teacher's single connection receives ~80 bundles a second in the hub design. This assumes Ably's "outbound" means delivery to the client, which the page does not spell out.

**Security and upkeep.** The relay must accept only signed-in students. Supabase publishes a JWKS endpoint so asymmetric tokens can be verified "without calling Supabase servers", and warns that the legacy shared secret "can be used to impersonate your users" if leaked ([Supabase JWTs](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/auth/jwts); [Supabase JWT signing keys](https://supabase.com/features/jwt-signing-keys)). GDScript has no JOSE library, so the simplest Godot-relay check is one REST call to `auth/v1/user` per connection, followed by a session-membership check. That is an inference, and whether Godot's `Crypto` class can verify ES256 is unverified. Platform redeploys and restarts drop WebSockets, so clients need keepalives and reconnect with backoff ([Render WebSockets](https://render.com/docs/websocket); [Cloudflare WebSockets](https://developers.cloudflare.com/network/websockets/)). Because the relay carries only movement and display copies of progress, **an outage degrades smoothness, never scores or gems**.

## Most cheap bandwidth tricks are already built; packing bytes is what remains

The teacher hub already uses the biggest standard techniques. It **batches**: the N-to-1-to-N hub replaced an all-to-all broadcast that needed about 30 × 7 × 31 ≈ 6,500 deliveries/s at 30 players. It **suppresses idle senders** and **skips empty ticks**, as Toontown and Among Us do ([Panda3D broadcaster](https://github.com/panda3d/panda3d/blob/master/direct/src/distributed/cDistributedSmoothNodeBase.cxx); [Among Us decompiled](https://github.com/polusgg/assets-unity/blob/master/Assets/OfficialAssets/Scripts/CustomNetworkTransform.cs)). It sends **changed-only snapshots**, **halves its send rate on a weak line**, as Overwatch does ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/overwatch-netcode-tick-rate-60hz/)), and gives each bundle **0.3 s of overlap**. That overlap is a bounded version of Tribes sending each move three times. Tribes also warned that resending everything unacknowledged "caused a negative feedback loop" on slow links ([Frohnmayer & Gift, Tribes networking](https://www.gamedevs.org/uploads/tribes-networking-model.pdf)). What remains is listed below, priced for 40 phones against today's ~185-203 messages/s.

| Technique | Supabase msg/s at 40 | Bytes | Delay effect | Solo effort | Failure modes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Message and byte figures are arithmetic from the project's measurements.

Fiedler's compression series shows how far packing can go. Quantization cut each object from 321 bits to 80, and delta plus index encoding took his scene from 17.37 Mbit/s to **256 kbit/s, about 1.5% of the start** ([Gaffer On Games](https://gafferongames.com/post/snapshot_compression/)). Here the measured JSON costs about 87 bytes per hero (2.6 KB / 30). Packed into 6-12 bytes, that is an 82-93% cut. **Under Supabase, smaller payloads do not reduce the count**, because Supabase counts messages, not bytes. They do save phone data, Wi-Fi airtime and parse time. **On a relay, packing becomes mandatory.** Kept as JSON at today's ~87 bytes per hero, a 10 Hz snapshot of 40 heroes is ~3.5 KB. That means ~35 KB/s into each phone (~125 MB per class-hour) and ~5 GB/h out of the server. Packed to 6 bytes per hero, it is ~240-330 bytes on the wire: **~2.4-3.3 KB/s per phone (~9-12 MB per class-hour) and 0.34-0.47 GB/h of server egress**, about $1/month on Fly ([Fly.io pricing](https://docs.fly.io/about/pricing); wire overhead from [RFC 6455](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6455#section-5.2) and [RFC 8446](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8446#section-5.2)). Today's hub uses ~10 KB/s per phone at 40 players (~37 MB per class-hour). **A packed 10 Hz relay therefore uses a quarter to a third of today's mobile data while refreshing three times as often.** Godot's `PackedByteArray.encode_u16`/`decode_u16` cover the codec without plugins. It is unverified whether Supabase Broadcast accepts raw binary or needs base64.

Dead reckoning ranks low despite its savings. DIS, the military-simulator standard, sends an update only when the shared prediction drifts past a threshold, plus a heartbeat at least every 5 s. Aronson warns that large thresholds produce "noticeable jerkiness" and that "if all the entities in a simulation behave unpredictably all the time, dead reckoning offers little gain" ([Aronson 1997](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~cs4344/0607s1/deadreckoning.pdf)). A thumb on an analog stick is that unpredictable entity. At 150 px/s and 0.3-0.5 s of latency, each turn would need a 45-75 px correction. The project has already watched that approach fail once. It becomes attractive only if the stick is quantized to 8 directions and corrections are blended, and even then it saves uploads, not the snapshot fan-out that dominates Supabase's count.

Sources: [Godot WebSocketPeer](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_websocketpeer.html); [Godot ENetMultiplayerPeer](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_enetmultiplayerpeer.html); [Godot WebRTC](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/networking/webrtc.html); [Fly.io UDP](https://docs.fly.io/networking/udp-and-tcp); [Cloudflare TURN](https://developers.cloudflare.com/realtime/turn/index.md); [IETF WebTransport draft](https://www.ietf.org/archive/id/draft-ietf-webtrans-http3-15.html).

The classroom-app industry has already settled this. Wayground (formerly Quizizz) requires "WebSockets for socket.wayground.com on port 443" and publishes a domain allow-list and a check page ([Wayground network guide](https://wayground.com/home/domain-safelisting)). Kahoot warns that firewalls "automatically close connections if they remain open" and that SSL inspection replaces its certificates and breaks authentication ([Kahoot support](https://support.kahoot.com/hc/en-us/articles/115003198708-How-to-avoid-connectivity-issues)). UDP-dependent products show the alternative. Google Meet falls back to TCP at lower quality when UDP is blocked ([Google Meet network prep](https://knowledge.workspace.google.com/admin/meet/prepare-your-network-for-meet-meetings-and-live-streams)), and Discord voice fails outright on networks that block UDP ([Discord support](https://support.discord.com/hc/en-us/articles/115001310031)). Client isolation, common on guest and BYOD networks and always on in Meraki's NAT mode ([Cisco Meraki](https://documentation.meraki.com/MR/Design_and_Configure/Firewall_and_Traffic_Shaping/Wireless_Client_Isolation)), blocks phone-to-laptop traffic. That, together with Android hotspots capped at about 10 devices ([Pixel Help](https://support.google.com/pixelphone/answer/2812516?hl=en)), confirms the client's rejection of LAN. A cloud relay on 443 avoids all of these problems. It adds one domain that school IT may need to allow-list, so the app should get a network-check screen like Wayground's.

WebSocket has a known cost: TCP's head-of-line blocking. A lost segment holds back everything behind it for at least one round trip and "commonly... 2*RTT" ([Gaffer On Games, UDP vs TCP](https://gafferongames.com/post/udp_vs_tcp/)). Linux, and therefore Android, uses a 200 ms minimum retransmission timeout ([netdev](https://lists.openwall.net/netdev/2010/06/04/6)), and the timeout doubles on each repeat loss ([RFC 6298](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6298)). With sparse 2-10 Hz traffic, one loss probably costs a 250-600 ms stall. A double loss costs 0.6-1.8 s, which hits about 0.25% of segments at 5% independent loss (inference; real Wi-Fi loss comes in bursts). **Today's ~1 s buffer hides most single stalls. A 0.3-0.5 s relay buffer will show some as a brief freeze followed by catch-up.** That is the honest trade-off of lower delay. The mitigations are already partly in place: let the adaptive buffer grow on a bad line, extrapolate briefly when it runs dry (Valve's approach), always send absolute positions, and jump to the newest sample after a stall instead of replaying the backlog.

Classroom Wi-Fi capacity is about airtime, not bytes. K-12 guidance plans for about 30 active devices per classroom AP ([CommScope](https://www.commscope.com/blog/2016/k-12-wi-fi-one-ap-per-classroom-is-it-necessary)). Kahoot suggests 10-15 Mbps up and 20-30 Mbps down per ~100 players ([Kahoot support](https://support.kahoot.com/hc/en-us/articles/115003198708-How-to-avoid-connectivity-issues)), and this game's total of a few Mbit/s at most is far below that. One snapshot per tick per phone, rather than one message per hero, keeps the frame count low. No published classroom latency or jitter measurements exist. On mobile data, only 45-55% of Philippine tests per operator met Opensignal's "consistent quality" thresholds in October 2025 ([unbox.ph summarizing Opensignal](https://unbox.ph/news/dito-best-network-philippines-october-2025/)). Carriers reportedly use carrier-grade NAT, so only outbound connections such as `wss://` to a cloud server are reliable. That evidence is low-authority ([Plex forum](https://forums.plex.tv/t/port-forwarding-blowing-my-mind/549010?page=2)). Android's Doze "suspends network access" when the screen is off ([Android Doze](https://developer.android.com/training/monitoring-device-state/doze-standby)). A locked phone should count as away and reconnect immediately on resume. Reconnects need **full jitter**: `sleep = random(0, min(cap, base × 2^attempt))`. With 100 contending clients, full jitter cut total calls by more than half ([AWS Architecture Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/architecture/exponential-backoff-and-jitter/)). The Supabase and Phoenix defaults step deterministically through 1, 2, 5 and 10 s ([Supabase heartbeat docs](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/troubleshooting/realtime-heartbeat-messages)), so 40 phones that lose the same access point would otherwise reconnect in lockstep. Phoenix drops in-flight pushes on a dead socket ([Phoenix socket.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/phoenixframework/phoenix/main/assets/js/phoenix/socket.js)), so every rejoin should be followed by a full snapshot.

## Everyone can see everyone; teammate-only views save nothing here

The intuitive cheaper design, "teammates live, others slow", fails on arithmetic in this setup. **Every hero is a teammate of four other students**, so every phone must still upload at the fast rate its teammates need; team priority can only trim the downlink. On Supabase, which bills 1 send plus 1 per receiver ([Supabase billing](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/platform/manage-your-usage/realtime-messages)), splitting today's 3 Hz class snapshot into six 3 Hz team snapshots plus a 1 Hz class snapshot costs 3 × 6 × 6 + 41 = **149 messages/s, against 123 today**. Per-viewer screen culling costs 240 (my arithmetic). On a relay that sends each phone its own packet, team priority would cut updates per phone by ~69% (team at 5 Hz, others at 1 Hz). But packed snapshots are already only ~3 KB/s, so there is little left to save.

| Visibility design | Supabase deliveries at 40 | Saving on a relay | Meets "all see each other"? | Precedent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Everyone live (today, and the relay) | 123/s snapshot fan-out | baseline, ~3 KB/s per phone packed | yes | .io games, Among Us, Toontown |
| (b) Team live, other teams at low rate | ~149/s (+21%) | ~69-78% fewer updates | yes, coarser | Fiedler priority accumulator; MMO area of interest |
| (c) Only heroes on screen | ~240/s (+95%) | 50-83%, depending on clustering | only with edge markers | slither.io, Roblox streaming |
| (d) Tower arrival events only | ~99% fewer | ~all | no walking shown | today's fallback when the hub is gone |
| (e) One message per tap destination | ~80-95% fewer updates | small | yes, but needs tap controls | Club Penguin |
| (f) Send on change plus heartbeat | large saving while idle | large saving while idle | yes | Toontown, Among Us (already partly built) |

Sources: [Gaffer On Games](https://gafferongames.com/post/state_synchronization/); [Boulanger et al. 2006](https://www.sable.mcgill.ca/~clump/papers/boulanger-06-comparing.pdf); [Slither.io protocol](https://github.com/ClitherProject/Slither.io-Protocol/blob/master/Protocol.md); [Roblox streaming](https://create.roblox.com/docs/workspace/streaming); [Club Penguin fan server](https://github.com/solero/houdini/blob/master/houdini/handlers/play/player.py). Savings are back-of-envelope estimates.

The evidence on whether seeing classmates matters is suggestive rather than decisive. Gajadhar and colleagues found that a co-located co-player "significantly adds to the fun, challenge, and perceived competence" ([Gajadhar et al. 2008](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-88322-7_11)). Fall Guys players guessed they were playing with about **twice as many opponents as were present** ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/fall-guys/player-count)), so a crowd feels full without exact positions. Guild Wars 2 removed server-side culling in 2013 after late pop-in drew complaints, and replaced it with client-side model limits ([GW2 Wiki](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/Culling)). The lesson is that showing players in less detail is accepted, while hiding them is not. FFXIV added an option to hide players crowded around quest NPCs ([VGC](https://videogameschronicle.com/guide/ffxiv-hide-players-quest-npc)), which is relevant when 30 heroes stand at one tower. NumXventure's class of 40 is within every classroom cap found: Gimkit's 2D modes are capped at 60 ([Gimkit Help](https://help.gimkit.com/en/article/player-maximums-18mbcz0/)) and Minecraft Education at 30 ([Microsoft UK](https://ukstories.microsoft.com/2016/11/03/minecraft-education-edition-released-help-children-learn-schools/)). No study separates "seeing classmates move live" from "knowing they are playing". **The verdict: keep everyone visible at full rate on the relay.** Lower the rate per connection only for a phone on a weak line, as Tribes set per-client packet rates ([Tribes networking](https://www.gamedevs.org/uploads/tribes-networking-model.pdf)). Consider fading other teams' heroes near the player's own tower as a UI choice, not a network one.

**Recommended plan.** *Today:* do the dashboard check. If the project is on Free and its usage is over the documented limits, either turn on Pro with the cap off as a stopgap (~$62-143/month at the current rate) or bring the relay forward. *Days 1-2:* retune the jitter buffer (target ~2 intervals, extrapolation capped at ~0.25 s, jump to newest after a stall). Write one packed snapshot codec that the hub and the relay share. Add full-jitter reconnect and an immediate reconnect on `NOTIFICATION_APPLICATION_RESUMED`. *Days 3-7:* turn `LanHost` into a cloud host. That means rooms keyed by session ID, a token and membership check on connect, no UDP beacon, `wss://` through Fly's edge, packed snapshots of movers only at 10 Hz, each phone upload carrying its last 2-3 samples for loss cover, a per-connection rate drop on weak lines, and progress rows riding along as they do in the hub today. Phones connect to the relay first and switch to the Supabase hub after a few seconds of silence; the teacher's hub stays idle while the relay is healthy. Then run `tools/sim/lan_load.py --host <relay>` with 40 phones from the Philippines, and pilot one real class with an in-app network check, logging delay, stalls and reconnects. Choose the host by the load test: Fly `sin` if 40 phones stay within its CPU baseline, otherwise Railway Hobby or a DigitalOcean $6 droplet in Singapore. If the developer prefers TypeScript to paying $3-6, Cloudflare Durable Objects is the free variant of the same plan.

With the retuned buffer, expect ~0.8-1.0 s at 0.5 s bundles. If Supabase limits bind there, Pro with the cap off pays for headroom. If the hub is also gone, heroes stand at their towers as they do today, and answers and gems are unaffected throughout.

## Conclusion

The binding constraint was never server capacity or bandwidth. Forty phones at 10 Hz is about 1 Mbit/s once packed, and a laptop relayed it, even as JSON, on a twelfth of one core. The constraint is that the current transport bills by the message, which turns every gain in smoothness into a direct cost. Once that is clear, the parked LAN work looks different. The client rejected the venue (a hotspot in the classroom), not the code, and the same 160-line host running in a Singapore data centre gives the online, everyone-on-one-map game the client asked for, at roughly 10 Hz instead of 2. It also removes the teacher's laptop from the movement path, and with it the open risk that a minimized Game Controls window freezes the whole class.

The plan discrepancy deserves equal weight. If the project really is on Free, its load tests have been running at 1.6-3.5 times the documented per-second limit and would use up the monthly quota in about three class-hours. A move that takes positions off Supabase cuts Realtime traffic to a few messages a second, which would likely keep the project within Free's documented limits, so the relay may also be what keeps the rest of the backend free.
